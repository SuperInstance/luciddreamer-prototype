# Voice as Trust: Why Speaking to Agents Creates Different Safety Dynamics

## A Research Brief on Voice-First Interfaces and Human-Agent Trust Dynamics in Safety-Critical Domains

---

## Executive Summary

The PLATO maritime voice interface demonstrated empirically what oral cultures have known for millennia: voice is the native interface for embodied knowledge. Voice entry proved 44% faster than manual input, achieved 91% versus 78% completeness, and 57.5% of participants voluntarily chose voice for a subsequent task. Most tellingly, captains described voice interaction as "like radioing in" -- a natural, familiar act -- while manual entry "felt like paperwork." This research brief examines why voice creates measurably different trust dynamics than text-based interfaces in safety-critical, embodied domains. Drawing on human-agent trust theory, social presence research, cognitive load theory, and empirical studies across maritime, aviation, surgical, and emergency response contexts, we identify eight major findings that explain the trust-differential of voice interfaces. The core argument: voice achieves trust not merely through efficiency but through **modal parity** -- it eliminates the interface asymmetry that positions the human as supplicant to the machine. When both captain and agent speak, they meet as communicative equals, piggybacking on trust protocols established through centuries of oral knowledge transfer and decades of VHF radio discipline.

---

## Finding 1: Voice Carries Trust Signals That Text Cannot Transmit

Human cognition evolved to evaluate trustworthiness primarily through speech. Long before written communication existed, voice tone, cadence, and conversational rhythm served as signals of credibility and intent [^204^]. Short, Williams, and Christie's foundational Social Presence Theory (1976) established that communication media vary in their capacity to convey "the degree of salience of the other person in the interaction" [^261^][^264^]. Voice occupies a privileged position on this continuum: richer than text, more immediate than video, and uniquely capable of conveying paralinguistic cues -- pitch, prosody, emotional resonance -- that signal attentiveness, competence, and benevolence.

The ACM survey of voice in human-agent interaction confirms that trust emerges as one of the most commonly studied measures in voice interface research, appearing in 19% of studies alongside likeability (34%), anthropomorphism (24%), and social presence (15%) [^205^]. Recent machine learning approaches to trust estimation demonstrate that acoustic features -- particularly Mel-Frequency Cepstral Coefficients (MFCC) and formant features -- are among the most predictive variables for estimating user trust from conversational data, outperforming text-only models [^216^]. Lexical sentiment combined with acoustic formant features showed that trust is associated with positive valence and high emotional arousal -- dimensions that text interfaces simply cannot transmit [^216^].

For PLATO specifically, the "like radioing in" phenomenon represents what Foehr and Germelmann identify as the critical path to trust in voice-interaction technology: perceived personality of the voice interface creates anthropomorphic connection, which in turn activates social trust heuristics usually reserved for human-human interaction [^288^]. The maritime VHF radio is not merely a communication technology -- it is a **trust infrastructure**, governed by international protocols (ITU, IMO), monitored 24/7 by coast guards, and instantiated through decades of disciplined use [^208^][^212^]. PLATO piggybacks on this established trust protocol. When a captain speaks to PLATO, they are not learning a new interaction paradigm -- they are extending an existing, deeply trusted one.

## Finding 2: Cognitive Load Reduction Translates Directly to Trust Formation

PLATO's 44% speed improvement and 91% completeness rate are not merely efficiency metrics -- they are cognitive load indicators. Cognitive Load Theory, as developed by Sweller and extended by Paas and colleagues, identifies three forms of cognitive load: intrinsic (inherent task difficulty), extraneous (load generated by presentation format), and germane (productive processing that builds schemas) [^271^][^272^]. Manual entry in rough maritime conditions imposes substantial extraneous load: finding the device, positioning fingers, reading small screens, correcting typos, all while maintaining vessel awareness. Voice eliminates this extraneous load, freeing cognitive resources for the task itself.

In automotive contexts, voice-based interactions have been shown to reduce cognitive load by up to 40% compared to touchscreen navigation, even in the noisy, attention-splitting environment of vehicle operation [^238^]. Similarly, studies comparing speech-based to manual interfaces in driving contexts consistently find that while speech interactions do impose some cognitive cost, the self-reported workload is lower and perceived safety higher for speech compared to manual operations [^252^]. The relationship between cognitive load and trust operates through what Lee and See term **performance** -- the most direct basis for trust in automation: "performance refers to the current and historical operation of the automation and includes such characteristics as reliability, predictability and ability" [^263^]. When voice reduces the effort required to achieve a goal, the system's apparent performance increases, and trust rises accordingly.

The completeness metric is equally significant. At 91% versus 78%, voice captures more complete information, meaning the agent has better data to work with, producing better outcomes, which further reinforces trust. This creates a **virtuous trust cycle**: reduced effort leads to higher compliance leads to better data leads to better agent performance leads to increased trust leads to further compliance. Manual entry, by contrast, initiates a vicious cycle: higher effort leads to lower compliance leads to incomplete data leads to poorer agent performance leads to trust erosion.

## Finding 3: Voice Eliminates Interface Asymmetry -- Creating Modal Parity

Perhaps the most theoretically significant dimension of voice interaction is what we term **modal parity**: when both human and agent communicate through the same modality -- speech -- the power asymmetry inherent in graphical interfaces dissolves. In a text-based system, the human must translate their embodied, situated knowledge into the machine's symbolic representation system (typing, menus, forms). The human adapts to the machine. In voice interaction, both parties speak. The machine has adapted to the human.

This parity matters profoundly for trust. Lee and See's integrative model of trust in automation identifies three bases of trust: performance (what the system does), process (how the system operates), and purpose (why the system was designed) [^263^][^269^]. Text interfaces primarily convey performance information -- outputs, metrics, status indicators. Voice interfaces can convey all three: performance through accurate responses, process through conversational markers ("let me check that for you"), and purpose through prosodic warmth and contextual appropriateness. As one study of conversational AI trust found, participants rated trust higher when voice assistants acknowledged failures proactively, attempted repair, and maintained conversational continuity -- all signals of **process** and **purpose**, not merely performance [^285^].

The "paperwork" framing from PLATO's captains is diagnostic. Paperwork is bureaucratic overhead -- the domain of hierarchical organizations demanding compliance from subordinates. Radio communication is peer-to-peer -- the domain of coordinated action among equals. When PLATO's captains say voice is "like radioing in," they are reporting a fundamental shift in how they perceive the agent: not as a system to be operated, but as a partner to be communicated with. This maps precisely onto what Lowenthal and others identify as the shift from perceiving technology as a tool to perceiving it as a social presence [^266^].

## Finding 4: Maritime Knowledge Has Always Been Oral -- Voice Digitizes Without Formalizing

Maritime knowledge transfer has relied on oral traditions for millennia. Research on the Riau Archipelago maritime communities documents how "oral literature, particularly folktales and pantun, serves as the primary medium for transferring knowledge about maritime life" -- including navigation techniques, sea conditions, and fishing strategies passed down through generations [^215^]. Similarly, Chalmers research on maritime pilot training found that informal interactions -- meals, coffee breaks, storytelling -- "served as key nodes for knowledge transfer," particularly for tacit knowledge that resists formal documentation [^218^].

PLATO's voice interface respects this epistemological tradition. It does not force captains to translate their embodied, situated knowledge into predefined form fields. Instead, it allows them to narrate their observations, concerns, and intentions in natural language -- the same way they would brief a fellow mariner. This is what we term **digitization without formalization**: the knowledge becomes machine-processable without losing its natural, contextual, embodied character.

This finding has broad implications for what Suchman and Dourish's situated action perspective would predict: knowledge in embodied domains is irreducibly situated, and interfaces that respect this situatedness will achieve higher trust and adoption than interfaces that impose abstract formalization. The 23/40 participants who chose voice for their second task were voting with their cognitive effort, preferring the interface that respected their existing communication ecology.

## Finding 5: Voice Degrades Gracefully -- Trust Does Not Collapse at 63%

PLATO's voice recognition degrades to 63% accuracy in heavy rain -- a critical finding that raises the question: when voice fails, does trust collapse? The evidence suggests it does not, provided the system handles failure appropriately.

Research on trust repair in voice assistants found that users naturally repair trust by relying on the assistant for different tasks following a failure, or the same task but in different contexts [^285^]. Critically, proactive acknowledgment of failure ("I'm having trouble hearing you because of the rain") increases perceptions of intelligence, and apology-based mitigation strategies effectively restore perceptions of likeability and competence [^285^]. This suggests that the attempt at voice communication itself maintains trust -- the captain sees the system is trying to understand, not ignoring them.

Furthermore, Krantz et al.'s finding that speech ability mitigates trust loss during robot failures is directly relevant [^288^]: when a robot could speak, trust levels remained stable even during faulty behavior; when the robot could not speak, trust dropped during failures. The implication for PLATO is profound: simply possessing voice capability -- even imperfect voice capability -- may buffer trust against performance degradation. The captain knows the system is listening. That knowledge sustains trust through transient failure.

Lee and See's concept of **calibration** -- the correspondence between trust and actual system capabilities -- is the operative framework here [^263^]. The goal is not perfect recognition in all conditions; it is appropriate calibration of trust. If captains understand that voice works less well in heavy rain (just as VHF radio degrades in certain atmospheric conditions), they can adjust their reliance accordingly without abandoning the system entirely. This calibrated trust -- trust matched to capability -- is exactly what Lee and See identify as the design target for safe automation [^269^].

## Finding 6: Voice Creates Social Presence That Activates Interpersonal Trust Heuristics

Voice interfaces trigger what Reeves and Nass identified as the "Computers Are Social Actors" (CASA) paradigm: humans respond to computers with the same social heuristics they apply to human interaction [^288^]. Research on voice AI trust demonstrates that voice creates emotional context that text cannot replicate, and emotional context drives trust [^204^]. MIT research reveals that voice conversations activate multiple brain regions simultaneously -- areas responsible for emotion, memory, and social connection -- creating what neuroscientists call "multimodal memory anchors" [^210^]. People remember voice conversations 65% better than text exchanges, and trust formation happens up to 5x faster through voice than text [^210^].

The maritime context amplifies this effect. Maritime operations are fundamentally social. The captain communicates constantly -- with crew, with port authorities, with other vessels via VHF. Introducing a text-based interface into this oral ecology would be cognitively jarring, requiring the captain to shift from a social, embodied mode of communication to an abstract, symbolic one. Voice interfaces preserve the social fabric of maritime work.

Haziraj's research on anthropomorphism in AI voice assistants found that while gendered voices triggered stronger anthropomorphic attributions, what actually drove trust was not surface-level gender but "emotional tone, vocal expressiveness, contextual fit, and the perceived authenticity of the interaction" [^287^]. This suggests that PLATO's success does not require a highly anthropomorphized voice -- it requires an authentic, contextually appropriate voice that matches the operational culture of maritime communication. The "Alaskan accent" mentioned in the Future User Manual whitepaper is not a gimmick; it is a **contextual authenticity signal** that increases perceived trustworthiness by matching the user's communication environment.

## Finding 7: The Trust Advantages of Voice Extend Across Safety-Critical Domains

The trust dynamics observed with PLATO in maritime contexts replicate across multiple safety-critical, embodied domains:

**Aviation** represents the most mature application of voice trust infrastructure. Direct Voice Input (DVI) is deployed in the Eurofighter Typhoon, F-35 Lightning II, Dassault Rafale, and Saab JAS 39 Gripen -- with the explicit goal of reducing pilot workload during high-stakes operations [^249^]. The European VOICI project achieved 95% word recognition rate in realistic noisy cockpit environments and found that "as crew's tasks become more and more complicated, it is hugely important that we put in place natural interfaces that help to simplify these tasks" [^244^]. The Goose AI Voice CoPilot for general aviation explicitly markets itself as reducing workload to allow pilots to "focus on flying" [^253^]. The trust mechanism is identical to PLATO's: voice preserves attention for the primary task while maintaining communicative flow with the agent.

**Surgery** presents a different but equally compelling trust case. Voice control in operating rooms aligns directly with sterile technique -- surgeons cannot touch non-sterile devices during procedures [^241^]. Voice-controlled surgical robot assistants using OpenAI's Whisper model demonstrate that modern speech recognition can handle the "noisy and dynamic environment of an operating room" [^246^]. However, challenges remain: background noise from anesthesia machines, ventilators, and suction equipment creates what Dr. Daniel Eun identifies as a fundamentally communication-challenged environment where "high-quality audio is essential" [^247^]. The VoiceCheck system for surgical safety checklists found that "even passive use (reading only) proved helpful" during "Team Time-out" phases, suggesting that voice support for structured communication protocols directly enhances safety [^250^].

**Emergency Response** perhaps most closely mirrors maritime conditions. The DHS-JHU DAISI system was explicitly designed for voice-activated operation in "noisy operational environments" where current speech recognition "quickly fail[s] when the surrounding background noise increases" [^245^]. Field testing with Howard County Fire & Rescue validated voice-activated mayday alerts, hydrant location queries, friction loss calculations, and hazardous materials lookups -- all hands-free, all in high-stakes conditions. As Battalion Chief Stanley Wurzburger stated, "DAISI quite literally can make the worst day of a firefighter's career a little bit easier to get through" [^245^]. This statement captures the essence of voice-mediated trust: when everything is going wrong, the ability to simply speak and be understood is not a convenience -- it is a lifeline.

## Finding 8: Voice Interface Trust Has a Dark Side -- Overtrust and the Calibrated Reliance Imperative

The research on trust in automation carries a consistent warning: inappropriate trust -- both overtrust and undertrust -- can be as dangerous as no trust at all [^263^][^269^]. Parasuraman and Riley's research on automation-induced complacency demonstrates that highly capable systems may invite intentional misuse due to overtrust, potentially leading to failure to properly supervise system operation [^273^].

Voice interfaces, by virtue of their social presence and fluency, may be particularly susceptible to overtrust. Torre et al. found that a "smiling voice" increased and sustained trust "despite evidence against trustworthiness" -- a phenomenon that could lead to dangerous overreliance in safety-critical contexts [^288^]. The 63% rain degradation figure for PLATO is therefore not merely a technical specification but a **trust calibration signal**: users must understand that voice recognition, like all automation, has bounded capability.

The design implication is what Lee and See term **appropriate reliance** -- trust calibrated to actual system capability through what they identify as three components: proper calibration (accurate knowledge of capabilities), resolution (precision in differentiating capability levels), and specificity (trust targeted to specific functions) [^263^]. PLATO's multimodal design -- voice as primary, manual as fallback -- supports appropriate reliance by making capability boundaries visible. When voice fails, manual entry remains possible. This **graceful degradation pathway** prevents the kind of trust collapse that occurs when systems fail catastrophically without alternative.

## Implications for Voice-First AI Design

This research synthesis yields six design principles for voice-first AI in safety-critical, embodied domains:

**1. Piggyback on existing trust protocols.** PLATO succeeds because it maps to VHF radio communication patterns. Designers should identify the existing oral trust infrastructures in their target domains and align voice interaction patterns with them.

**2. Prioritize modal parity over anthropomorphic excess.** The goal is not to make the agent seem human but to make the interaction seem equal. Both parties speak. Both parties listen. The interface does not position the human as supplicant.

**3. Design for graceful degradation with trust repair.** Voice will fail. The design question is not how to prevent failure but how to maintain trust through failure. Proactive acknowledgment, contextual explanation ("heavy rain is making it hard to hear you"), and seamless fallback pathways are essential.

**4. Match voice characteristics to operational culture.** The "Alaskan accent" matters not as a novelty but as a contextual authenticity signal. Voice design should match the linguistic and prosodic norms of the operational community.

**5. Monitor and support trust calibration.** Voice interfaces should provide subtle signals of confidence and capability boundaries, enabling users to develop appropriate rather than excessive trust. The system should help users know when to trust and when to verify.

**6. Preserve situated knowledge in its natural form.** Do not force formalization. Allow practitioners to narrate, describe, and explain in their own language. The system should adapt to the user's knowledge ecology, not demand that the user adapt to the system's.

---

## Conclusion

PLATO's finding that captains described voice as "like radioing in" is the key to understanding voice-mediated trust. Voice does not merely offer a faster input method -- it fundamentally restructures the human-agent relationship from operator-and-system to speaker-and-listener. It eliminates interface asymmetry, piggybacks on centuries of oral knowledge tradition and decades of VHF radio discipline, reduces cognitive load, creates social presence, and enables trust repair through conversational continuity.

The 44% speed improvement, 91% completeness rate, and 57.5% voluntary adoption rate are not independent metrics -- they are manifestations of a single underlying dynamic: voice respects the embodied, situated, oral nature of maritime expertise. When technology respects the human's way of knowing, trust follows naturally. This principle extends far beyond maritime domains to any context where skilled practitioners operate in demanding physical environments with limited attention and high stakes: emergency response, surgery, aviation, construction, and beyond. In these domains, voice is not merely an interface modality -- it is a trust modality.

The PLATO project's claim that "voice is the native interface for embodied knowledge in maritime domains" is thus not a marketing slogan but an empirically grounded theoretical proposition with broad applicability across safety-critical human-agent systems. Future research should systematically compare trust development trajectories across voice, text, and multimodal interfaces in varied safety-critical domains, examine trust calibration over extended operational use, and investigate how voice interface characteristics (prosody, accent, response latency, repair strategies) differentially affect the performance, process, and purpose dimensions of trust identified by Lee and See's foundational framework.
